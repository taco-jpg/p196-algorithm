01 · THE PROBLEM

Add a number to its reverse. Repeat until it reads the same both ways.

Most numbers become palindromes fast: 89 takes 24 steps. 196 has never been seen to reach one. The record runs (Romain Dolbeau: 1 billion steps in 2011, a 1-billion-digit number in 2015) found nothing, and nobody has a proof either way. So the only way forward is to compute, exactly, one step at a time.

Try:
Digits vs. step for the current run. 196 grows about 0.414 digits per step (Dolbeau's 1e9 steps gave 413,930,770 digits), so the work per step keeps growing. Your run is past 270 million digits.
Start here: One step means one complete addition. A digit is one character in the answer. The number of digits grows slowly; it is not the number of steps completed.

02 · FOLDING · ALREADY IN THE FIRST KERNEL

Why do the same small sum twice?

Look at 196 + 691. The left column adds 1 + 6, while the right column adds 6 + 1. Both begin with 7. The engine can reuse that matching sum.

01 / 04 · MATCH THE ENDS
The idea to remember: Store each matching pair sum once, not twice. Incoming carries can still make the two final digits different.
Original interactive folding diagram + the full equations

Position j of x + reverse(x) adds dj and dn−1−j. Position n−1−j adds the same two digits. So both positions start from the same pair sum Sj = dj + dn−1−j (0 to 18) and differ only in the carry that arrives: aj from below, bj on the mirrored side. Half the values, and a free palindrome test: the next number is a palindrome exactly when aj = bj for every pair.

S ≥ 10: makes a carry S = 9: passes a carry on S ≤ 8: stops a carry ● carry = 1

03 · CARRIES AND NINES

Why does one extra 1 travel so far?

A carry is the extra 1 passed to the next column when a sum reaches 10. A sum of 9 is special: if a 1 arrives, 9 + 1 = 10, so that carry keeps traveling.

The idea to remember: A string of 9s acts like a bridge for a carry. The GPU finds the same carries with a machine-level addition instead of walking through every digit in a slow loop.
Original carry lab: edit all the sums and inspect the binary masks

The carry into pair j+1 is aj+1 = Gj | (Pj & aj): generate if S ≥ 10, propagate if S = 9. That chain is the only long-distance effect in a step, and it is exactly the chain a binary adder already computes. So the engine resolves a whole word of carries with one integer addition instead of a loop. Tap a pair to select it, then set its value.

Bit 0 is on the left here, so carries move to the right. On the GPU a word is 32 pairs wide. If the carry coming into a word is unknown, the engine scans to the nearest word that is not entirely 9s (chance about 10−32 per word). No cutoff: a run of a million 9s would still come out right.

04 · BIT-PLANES · V4

How can one operation handle 32 pairs?

A bit is just an on/off switch. Five switches worth 16, 8, 4, 2, 1 can represent any pair sum between 0 and 18. Rearrange those switches into rows, and the GPU can test many sums together.

05 · 128 STEPS PER MEMORY TRIP · V5

Why keep fetching the same data?

Think of GPU main memory as a storage room and faster shared memory as a worktable. Instead of making a round trip after every addition, load a piece, do several steps nearby, and write it back once.

Same arithmetic. Fewer trips to main memory.

The idea to remember: Time-blocking does not skip additions. It reduces data movement, while certification ensures the central results remain exact. If not, the engine redoes the uncertain work.
Original 128-step certification visualization and profiler note

Pair j of the next number depends only on its neighbours plus carries through runs of 9s. So a block can load a tile of pairs into fast shared memory and run K steps (production: 128) before writing back. The catch: the tile's edges don't know what their outside neighbours are doing. Wrongness creeps in from each edge, one pair per step, and faster across a run of 9s. Each block tracks the interval it can prove exact. If that interval no longer covers the pairs the block owns, the block reports failure and the host redoes that stretch with the slow per-step kernel. The result is exact either way.

Per-step kernel (v4): every step streams every pair through GPU memory: trips for K steps.
Time-blocked (v5 on): one load and one store per K steps. In production the GPU's main memory sits about 1.8% busy (Nsight Compute).

Schematic of the certification rule: shrink one pair per side per step, plus any run of 9s at the edge. Production halos are wide enough that this check essentially never fails, but correctness never depends on that. Growth bits come from a small certified "prefix" tile, and every step's palindrome status is counted per block.

06 · INSIDE ONE BLOCK · V6R3 → V8

Why have a team meeting after every step?

A GPU thread is a worker. In v6r3, the workers exchange data and wait for the team after every step. That stop is called a barrier. In v8, a worker keeps a private copy in registers and can do several steps before the next meeting.

The idea to remember: Meeting less often helps, but independence costs extra work and a shrinking safety margin. The winner must be measured, not just imagined.
Original v6r3 worker map, register timelines, and J=16 failure tests

Nsight Compute showed v6r2 was not short of arithmetic. It was waiting: the per-step barrier was the #1 stall, and on 51% of cycles no warp was ready to issue. v6r3 removed waiting in small ways and filled idle threads. v8 (the GPT-6 Astra idea, rebuilt inside our design) changed the shape of the work: each thread copies its 4 words plus 1 halo word per side into registers and runs J steps with no barrier and no shared memory, then syncs once.

Why J = 16 failed: the margin goes stale

A thread's window is 6 words = 192 pairs: 32 borrowed pairs on each side, 128 owned. Each step without a sync, the borrowed edge becomes less trustworthy by about one pair, and faster across a run of 9s. At the end of the window, the uncertainty must not have reached the owned words. If it did, the block fails and an 8,192-step stretch is redone with the slow kernel.

07 · BASE-5 PAIR SUMS · V8B OPTION

Could different labels make the math cheaper?

Instead of storing 17 only as ordinary binary, split it into 10 + 5 + 2. Now “is the sum at least 10?” has already been answered by one saved bit. The reverse-and-add problem itself still uses ordinary decimal numbers.

This option had formula checks, but no GPU speedup confirmed in the source snapshot. “Base-5” means an internal five-bit encoding, not changing the numeral base of the big integer.

The idea to remember: A representation can make the common questions simpler. Fewer instructions are only a hypothesis until an actual sustained GPU test wins.
Original five-bit comparison and complete base-5 formula

Same 5 bits, different meaning: M = S mod 5 (3 bits, 0–4), H = whether the last digit is ≥ 5, G = S ≥ 10. Then "makes a carry" is just the stored bit G, "passes a carry" (S = 9) is H & M₂, and the new digit is M + 5H + c − 10·P·c: G cancels out. Counted, that is about a quarter fewer logic operations in steps where the number doesn't grow, roughly even in growth steps. v8b converts at window load and store, so checkpoints stay binary.

10 · WHAT'S NEXT

Milestones and the one hard deadline

One billion steps means one billion additions. One billion digits means the result is a billion characters long. These milestones are not interchangeable.

The dates below are projections recorded in the original October 13, 2026 snapshot, not a live status report.

Projected at v8 speed with no downtime; 1 billion steps is expected around this evening (Pacific). The red date is the hard one: v8 refuses to go past 2.147 billion digits, so production must be on v8b first.

Lessons that held up

  • The math gave the big wins. Folding, bit-planes and time-blocking are why memory sits at 1.8% busy and why we're ~20× ahead of schoolbook on the same chip (estimate).
  • The 35 W cap prices everything in energy. Filling idle cycles drops the clock. More blocks per SM lost 8.7%; v8's 1.5× extra arithmetic took most of the 1.5× it saved in waiting.
  • Fewer instructions ≠ faster. v7r cut instructions per word 1.9× and lost 4%.
  • Exactness is non-negotiable. Every step's palindrome status is checked, no carry cutoffs, uncertified work replays. A v7 bug that gave 12 wrong results out of 78 was caught by tests before it ever ran on your GPU.

Open items

  • v8 in production: passed (+23.49% sustained, 0 replays); the switch is your call.
  • v8b on the GPU: gate, the 2.147e9-digit test, then the A/B of 288 threads, J=12 and base-5. Must be in production before about Nov 15.
  • Record checks: ISF dumps at milestones, mod-9 check on every archive, weekly per-step cross-check (the laptop GPU has no ECC memory).
  • The biggest lever left is hardware: no kernel change on this laptop is likely to match a desktop GPU without the 35 W cap.
