# Fixed-modulus arithmetic cannot isolate a decimal Lychrel orbit

This note proves an obstruction to one certificate class. It does not prove
that a decimal Lychrel number exists, nor that all reverse-and-add orbits hit
palindromes. The statements concern ordinary finite positive integers.

## 1. An explicit exact-length residue theorem

Let M >= 1 and factor M = u q, where u = 2^alpha 5^beta and gcd(q,10)=1.
Set k=max(1,alpha,beta). If q>1, let r be the multiplicative order of 10
modulo q; if q=1, set r=1 by convention. Define

    L0(M) = 2 k + 4 q r.

**Theorem.** For every L >= L0(M), the residue class a modulo M contains an
ordinary positive L-digit decimal palindrome if and only if both conditions
hold:

1. It is not the case that 10 divides both M and a.
2. If L is even, gcd(M,11) divides a.

The same single bound L0 works for every larger L, without a condition on L
modulo r. The bound is deliberately generous, not an asserted optimal bound.

### Necessity

A positive palindrome has nonzero units digit. An even-length decimal
palindrome is divisible by 11. These imply the two conditions.

### Sufficiency: choose the fixed outer digits

Choose A with 0 <= A < 10^k, A congruent to a modulo u, and 10 not dividing A.
Here is an explicit choice. Start with A=a mod u, with the usual A=0 when
u=1. If 10 divides A, replace A by A+u. When 10 divides u, condition 1
ensures that this replacement is unnecessary. When 10 does not divide u,
the replacement has nonzero units digit and is still less than 10^k:
u is 1, a power of 2, or a power of 5, and 2u <= 10^k.

Write A as a zero-padded block of k digits. Put this block at the low end
of an L-digit word and its reversal at the high end; put zero in every
remaining position. Denote the resulting palindrome by C. It has exactly
L digits because A has nonzero units digit. Since u divides 10^k, C is
congruent to a modulo u. If L is even, C is divisible by 11.

### Sufficiency: use disjoint pools of mirrored interior positions

Number positions from 0 at the units end. A digit 1 in positions i and
L-1-i contributes

    c_i = 10^i + 10^(L-1-i).

Use the two disjoint intervals

    I0 = { k, ..., k+q r-1 },
    I1 = { k+q r, ..., k+2 q r-1 }.

The bound on L implies every position in these intervals is strictly below
the center: i <= floor((L-2)/2). Thus no mirror pair overlaps another or the
fixed outer blocks. I0 contains q positions congruent to 0 modulo r, and I1
contains q positions congruent to 1 modulo r. If r=1, the two residue labels
coincide, but the pools are still disjoint. This explicitly handles order 1.

Modulo q, their respective weights are

    c0 = 1 + 10^(L-1),
    c1 = 10 + 10^(L-2).

They satisfy the exact integer identity

    10 c1 - c0 = 99.

Therefore gcd(q,c0,c1) divides 99. Since c0 is 2 modulo 3, this gcd has
no factor 3. Modulo 11, c0 is 0 for even L and 2 for odd L. Consequently

    gcd(q,c0,c1) = gcd(q,11) if L is even, and 1 if L is odd.

In particular, even arbitrarily high powers of 11 in q create exactly one
mandatory factor of 11 in this gcd, never a higher power.

Put g=gcd(q,c0,c1). The subgroup generated by c0 and c1 in Z/qZ consists
exactly of the multiples of g. Conditions 1-2 and the already fixed C imply
g divides a-C. Hence there are integers x,y, which can be chosen in
{0,...,q-1}, such that

    x c0 + y c1 = a-C (mod q).

Set the digit to 1 at any x of the indicated I0 pairs and any y of the
indicated I1 pairs. Keep every other free digit zero. The result P remains
an L-digit palindrome. Every newly used position is at least k, so P=C=a
modulo u. By construction P=a modulo q. The Chinese remainder theorem gives
P=a modulo M. For q=1 there is no second congruence to solve; take x=y=0.
This proves the theorem.

## 2. No arithmetic trap using a fixed modulus and even exact length

Let T(n)=n+reverse10(n), where n is a positive ordinary integer and reversal
uses its ordinary decimal representation. Let ell(n) be its digit length.

**Corollary.** There is no nonempty set S of positive integers satisfying
all three conditions:

1. Membership in S depends only on the pair (ell(n), n mod M), for a single
   fixed M. The allowed residue set may depend arbitrarily on exact length.
2. T(S) is a subset of S.
3. S contains no positive decimal palindrome.

### Proof

Start from any n0 in S. Its iterates strictly increase because reverse10(n)
is positive. Their lengths are therefore unbounded. Each step increases
length by at most one, so the orbit eventually has even length. For any
length L,

    reverse10(n) = (-1)^(L-1) n (mod 11).

Thus T(n) is divisible by 11 at an even-length input. Once an iterate is
divisible by 11, all later iterates are too, regardless of their lengths.

Furthermore, if an input ends in zero, T(n) has as its units digit the
nonzero leading digit of n. Consequently two consecutive iterates cannot
both be divisible by 10.

Choose an iterate n beyond the first acquisition of 11-divisibility, with
ell(n) >= L0(M), and with 10 not dividing n. Such an iterate exists by the
two preceding paragraphs. Its residue class and exact length satisfy the
theorem's two compatibility conditions. There is therefore a palindrome P
with ell(P)=ell(n) and P=n modulo M. Condition 1 puts P in S, contrary to
condition 3. QED.

This also rules out a finite list of fixed moduli, a fixed finite list of
prime valuations with fixed finite truncation caps, arbitrary allowed residues depending on length modulo any fixed
integer, and restrictions to all sufficiently large lengths. Take the least
common multiple of the finitely many moduli. It does not rule out invariants
also using digit-language state, carries, boundary words, unbounded
valuations, or a modulus growing with length. It does not assert that an
actual orbit visits the constructed palindromes.

## 3. Quantitative requirement for a possible escape

Suppose membership at length L depends on n modulo a possibly varying M(L),
and the set is closed and palindrome-free. At every sufficiently late orbit
state with 10 not dividing n, the residue theorem forces

    L < 2 max(1,alpha(L),beta(L)) + 4 q(L) ord_q(L)(10),

with the q=1 convention above. Otherwise that state shares its length and
residue with a palindrome. This is a necessary condition only. It supplies
no closed invariant and is not claimed to be sharp.

## 4. Why the natural 10-adic extension does not supply a finite seed

There is an additional basic obstruction to treating T as a continuous map
on the 10-adic completion. Fix a positive finite n of length ell and any
c in {1,...,9}. For j>=ell, put n_j=n+c 10^j. Its ordinary leading digit is
c, and

    reverse10(n_j) = c + 10^(j-ell+1) reverse10(n).

Hence n_j tends 10-adically to n, whereas T(n_j) tends 10-adically to n+c.
Choosing c different from reverse10(n) modulo 10 proves discontinuity at n.
Thus no continuous self-map of the 10-adic integers restricts to ordinary
reverse-and-add on all finite positive integers. For example,

    n_j = 196 + 4*10^j,
    T(n_j) = 200 + 1091*10^(j-2),   j>=3,

so the input limit is 196 and the output limit is 200, while T(196)=887.

This does not preclude a different enlarged, two-sided digit state space.
It explains why a completion argument needs an explicit finite-support
realization theorem rather than merely an infinite-digit solution.

## 5. Published ingredient versus present derivation

Harminc and Sotak, "Palindromic Numbers in Arithmetic Progressions",
Fibonacci Quarterly 36(3) (1998), pp.259-262, prove that an ordinary
arithmetic progression contains infinitely many decimal palindromes unless
all of its members are divisible by 10. Original publisher PDF:

https://www.fq.math.ca/Scanned/36-3/harminc.pdf

That published theorem already implies the weaker no-trap statement when
membership depends only on n mod M. The explicit exact-length theorem,
the stronger exact-length no-trap corollary, and the quantitative observation
above are derived here with complete elementary proofs. No claim of
publication novelty or priority is made. The 10-adic counterexample is an
independent direct calculation.

There is also substantial prior exact-length work: Banks, Hart, and Sakata,
"Almost All Palindromes Are Composite", arXiv:math/0405056, Proposition 4.2,
proves exact-length equidistribution under gcd(M,990)=1 and in particular
implies sufficiently large exact-length residue saturation in that case.
The construction here must not be advertised as new in that special case.
The literature check located no full arbitrary-modulus support statement
with the elementary explicit bound above; that is not a novelty claim.
Primary source: https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0405056 .

The companion astra_modular_palindrome_builder.py implements the proof and
checks the resulting ordinary finite digit strings directly. Its controls
are verification of the construction, not a substitute for the proof.

On the recorded run all controls passed: 2,370 compatible classes for
moduli 1 through 40 were constructed; 90 incompatible cases were rejected;
142 selected cases covered pure 2/5 parts, order 1, and powers of 11 through
1331. The largest tested length was 1,288,411 digits. Separate ordinary
integer controls checked the reversal/modulo-11 identity and the no-two-zero
units fact for 99,999 inputs, and checked 17 instances of the 10-adic formula.
Results are in astra_modular_palindrome_builder_results.json.

## 6. Exactly which moduli permit a residue-only reversal update

**Theorem.** For a fixed M>=1, the residue reverse10(n) modulo M can be
determined from n modulo M and its exact digit length, for all positive
integers n, if and only if M divides 99. The necessity remains true if the
requirement is imposed only at every sufficiently large length. The same
classification holds for T(n) modulo M, since T(n)-n=reverse10(n).

**Sufficiency.** If M divides 99, then 10^2=1 modulo M, and termwise reversal
of the digit polynomial gives

    reverse10(n) = 10^(L-1) n (mod M),
    T(n) = 2 n (mod M) if L is odd,
    T(n) = 11 n (mod M) if L is even.

**Necessity, factors 2 and 5.** If p in {2,5} divides M, take a sufficiently
large L. Each of the two intervals of L-digit numbers beginning respectively
with 1 and 2 contains a representative of any given class modulo M. Their
reversals have different residues modulo p, namely 1 and 2, because 10=0
modulo p. Thus equal input residues at equal length do not determine the
reversal residue. Hence gcd(M,10)=1 is necessary.

**Necessity, the remaining factors.** Suppose gcd(M,10)=1 and take L large
enough that n=10^(L-1)+9, n+1, n+M, and n+M+1 all have length L. Choose
also L>=3. The number n ends in exactly one 9. Since M is not a multiple
of 10, n+M does not end in 9. Consequently the ordinary digit calculations
give

    reverse10(n+1)-reverse10(n) = 10^(L-2)-9*10^(L-1),
    reverse10(n+M+1)-reverse10(n+M) = 10^(L-1).

If reversal residues are determined by the stated data, these differences
are equal modulo M, because n and n+M have equal input residues and so do
n+1 and n+M+1. Thus M divides 99*10^(L-2). Coprimality gives M divides 99.

This explains why simply introducing modulo 121 (or another modulus outside
the divisors of 99) does not produce an autonomous arithmetic recurrence.
The reversal update then requires additional digit information. It does not
rule out using such a modulus together with that additional information.

## 7. A carry-sensitive escape condition, not a closed family

Using the established characterization of palindromic overflow, its
pre-addition symmetric column-sum word q must have outer coefficient 11
and all coefficients in {0,11}. Write q=11b coefficientwise. Then b is a
finite symmetric binary digit word. If the input width L is even, b is an
even-length decimal palindrome and its numerical value is divisible by 11.
Thus an overflow from even width to a palindrome necessarily satisfies

    121 divides T(n).

In particular v_11(T(n))=1 rules out this kind of hit. Odd input width only
forces the already known factor 11; its middle binary coefficient must be
zero because the original odd center coefficient q is even.

A constructive route using this observation would have to give a finite
seed and prove, for all future steps, BOTH (a) that no-overflow steps cannot
become palindromes (for example, an internal carry persists), and (b) that
the appropriate valuation/digit obstruction survives every overflow,
including odd-width overflows. Neither preservation claim has been proved
here. The observation is checkpointed as a possible ingredient only.
